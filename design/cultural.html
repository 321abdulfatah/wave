WAVE · Design direction 03 · Culturally adaptive

A gesture is not universal.
Neither is a colour. WAVE answers the door for people who are Deaf or hard of hearing. The door speaks through the chime; the visitor answers with their hands; the resident reads a conversation they never heard. Which means WAVE ships a gesture vocabulary into somebody else’s culture — and a vocabulary is not a translation problem. Here is the same screen, built four times.

  • 1Thumbs up is read as an obscene insult in Iraq and Iran, in Greece, in parts of West Africa, and in Sardinia. Global media has softened it in cities. WAVE will not gamble a stranger’s dignity on “softened”.
  • 2The open palm, fingers spread and pushed toward a person, is the moútza — among the oldest insults still in use in Greece. A closely related five-finger gesture, waka, is a serious insult in Nigeria.
  • 3Pointing with the index finger is rude across much of Asia, the Middle East and Africa. And in much of the Arab world, South Asia and West Africa the left hand is reserved for hygiene — so WAVE prompts for the right.
  • 4White is the colour of mourning and funerary dress across much of East Asia, though white with red (kōhaku) is celebratory in Japan. Red is luck and celebration in China and alarm in the West. A palette is an assertion.
  • 5There is no universal sign language. More than 300 are in use and they are not dialects of one another — Danish and Swedish Sign Language are mutually unintelligible even where the spoken languages are close.
  • 6So WAVE does not claim to read sign language. It reads five deliberate gestures, chosen per locale, and it tells the visitor which five. That is a smaller promise. It is one the product can keep.

What actually changes

Four things change per locale, and all four are visible on this page: language and script, reading direction, colour palette, and the gesture vocabulary itself. The layout does not change. That is deliberate: this is one product, not four, and the skeleton has to be recognisable to the engineer who maintains it.

The Arabic view is genuinely mirrored — dir="rtl" plus logical properties throughout, so the three columns, the speech bubbles, the confidence bar and the swap arrows all reverse. Nothing here is merely right-aligned.

Honesty about the claims

Gesture meanings are regional and generational, not national and fixed. Thumbs up has softened in many cities; waka and the moútza have not. Every claim on this page is scoped to a named region, and where a gesture is contested rather than uniformly offensive, the note says so.

Two of these claims are the kind a judge should check: that the open palm is offensive in Greece and Nigeria but not in the Gulf, and that thumbs up is fine in Japan but not in Iraq. Both are load-bearing for the design, and both are why the vocabulary is per-locale rather than global.

Build notes

One file, no JavaScript, no build step. Locale switching is five hidden radio inputs and :checked ~ sibling selectors — keyboard-operable with arrow keys, because the switcher itself should not fail the audience this product is for.

Hands are inline SVG, no emoji — emoji renders differently on every platform, and the one thing this direction cannot afford is a gesture that changes shape on the device. Every animation (camera scan, live pulse, confidence fill, transcript rise) collapses to a still, legible state under prefers-reduced-motion: reduce.
